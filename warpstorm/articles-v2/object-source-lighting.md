# Object Source Lighting for Miniatures: Simple OSL Guide

**Short answer:** OSL means painting light as if it comes from an object on the model: pick the light source, then tint every surface facing it with the light colour — strongest at the nearest point, fading with distance. Two glazes and one highlight colour are enough for a convincing effect. Reflection, not brightness, is the secret.

## What Is OSL — and Why It Elevates Your Models

Object Source Lighting is the painted illusion that a model *emits* light: a torch, a plasma coil, a warp portal. Done well, OSL makes viewers see light instead of paint. It's the single highest-impact technique in the hobby — and it's less about skill than about discipline: consistent light direction, controlled intensity, restrained coverage.

## Step 1: Determine Light Direction and Falloff

Fix three things before painting:

1. **Where** the light source sits on the model
2. **Which surfaces face it** — mentally trace a straight line from the source to every panel
3. **How far** the light reaches — falloff is fast; light rarely travels more than 10–15 mm convincingly

Mark the facing surfaces with a light pencil dot if you need to. OSL fails when light lands on surfaces that couldn't possibly see the source.

## Step 2: Layering Transparent Glazes

- Mix your glow colour as a **thin transparent glaze** (glaze medium or heavily thinned paint)
- Apply 1 glaze to the outermost zone of the lit area
- Apply 2–3 glazes on the mid zone
- Apply 4–6 glazes — or a stronger mix — on surfaces immediately adjacent to the source

Because glazes are transparent, the underlying paint still reads through: this is what makes OSL look like light on a surface rather than paint on top of it.

## Step 3: The Source Itself

Paint the emitting object at maximum brightness: base colour → bright tone → near-white core. The source must always be the brightest point of the model, or the whole illusion collapses.

## Step 4: Avoiding Common Over-Glowing Mistakes

- **The "spray tan" error** — tinting every surface equally. Light has direction; distance matters
- **Too saturated colour** — real light washes surfaces out; your glazes should lighten, not just colour
- **Light on impossible surfaces** — the underside of an arm facing away from the torch breaks the illusion instantly
- **Glow stronger than the source** — reflected light is always dimmer than the emitter

## FAQ

**Is OSL too advanced for beginners?**
Simple two-glaze OSL on one small light source is a beginner-accessible technique. Save the 20-glaze portal engines for model number fifty.

**What colour should I use for OSL?**
Match the light source's logic: fire = warm orange, plasma = blue/cyan, warp energy = violet or storm-green. The reflected colour must always match the source hue.

**Do I need an airbrush for OSL?**
No — an airbrush speeds up large surfaces, but glazes handle everything a tabletop model needs.

**How do I combine OSL with weathering?**
Weather first, glow second. OSL light passes over grime naturally — but paint order matters: pigments and chipping under glazes, never on top.
