3D Printed Terrain for Tabletop Games: Beginner Guide

Short answer: For tabletop terrain, choose FDM for large, sturdy pieces and resin for detail-rich small pieces. Print walls and ruins at 0.2 mm layer height with 8–10% infill, prime with filler-primed spray, and paint fast with contrast paints and drybrushing. One well-chosen print settings page saves you a spool of failed plastic.

FDM vs. Resin Printers for Terrain

For a first terrain project, FDM wins: terrain is big, and big resin prints fail, smell and cost.

Best Settings for Clean, Sturdy Sci-Fi Walls and Ruins

  • Layer height: 0.2 mm (0.28 mm acceptable for big blocks)
  • Infill: 8–10% gyroid — terrain doesn't need strength inside
  • Walls (perimeters): 3 for tabletop durability
  • Supports: tree supports for arches; orient flat faces upward to hide layer lines
  • Speed: slow the first layer; speed barely matters on long straight walls

The dirty secret of terrain printing: layer lines are free texture. Concrete, cast metal and adobe surfaces read better with slight line texture. Reserve ultra-fine printing for display pieces.

Primer and Painting Prep for 3D Prints

  1. De-string and sand contact faces so pieces sit flush
  2. Fill deep layer lines on display surfaces with filler primer (one coat, sand, repeat once)
  3. Spray prime dark grey or black — dark prime hides the seams that survive
  4. Glue terrain to a base (MDF or foam board) — unbased prints warp and chip

Quick Contrast-Painting Methods for Large Pieces

Terrain is where speed techniques shine — one wall is "miniature surface" a hundred times over:

  1. Spray the base colour (grey-brown for ruins) — a rattle can does in minutes what a brush does in hours
  2. Contrast-style paint over the whole piece with a large brush — one pass, no blending
  3. Drybrush the raised edges in a lighter stone tone — this is 80% of the visual payoff
  4. Wash the lowest third darker to ground the piece
  5. Optional: pigments on horizontal ledges for dust (see easy weathering)

A full table of terrain (a dozen pieces) is a two-weekend project with this method.

FAQ

Is 3D printing terrain cheaper than buying it?

After the printer investment, yes — dramatically. A kilo of filament yields months of terrain for the price of two retail boxes.

Do I need to worry about licenses on printed models?

Yes — check each file's license. Personal-use licenses forbid selling prints; many creators sell commercial licenses separately.

What size should tabletop terrain be?

Match your game's scale: for 28–32 mm miniatures, walls 50–80 mm tall and ruins footprint 100–200 mm work well. Print one test piece before committing to a full table.

How do I make terrain survive transport?

Print in modular sections with flat contact faces, base everything, and store in a box with foam dividers.
